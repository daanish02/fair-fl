2. PROBLEM SETUP / WHAT THE PAPER IS SOLVING
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The paper targets "performance fairness" in Federated Learning (FL): the
global model, once trained, can have very different accuracy across
different clients because client local data is heterogeneous (non-IID).
The paper's own definition (Definition 1, attributed to Li et al. [11],
i.e. the q-FedAvg paper):

  "Definition 1. (Fairness of performance distribution [11]). A model θ1
  is said to be fairer than θ2 if the accuracy of θ1 on the N clients
  {a1, a2, ..., aN} is more uniform than that of θ2 on the N clients."

The paper operationalizes this fairness notion as the VARIANCE of
per-client test accuracy across all clients (lower variance = fairer),
while also wanting to maintain a similar/competitive average accuracy.

Why plain FedAvg fails at this: FedAvg aggregates client models using a
weighted average based on the amount of local training data [15]. Under
heterogeneous (non-IID) data, this causes significant differences in
per-client model accuracy. The paper also argues that the standard
practice of selecting clients for each round based on the amount of
local data (or random selection) biases the global model toward
clients with more/majority data, further disadvantaging "vulnerable
clients" (clients with small or unusual/minority local data
distributions) — client selection itself is overlooked by prior fairness
reweighting work (q-FedAvg, FedFa, DRFL, etc.), which only reweight
aggregation but do not address *which* clients get selected to
participate each round. FCFL explicitly targets this gap by linking
BOTH client selection and aggregation reweighting to a single mechanism:
accumulated unfairness queues.


3. ALGORITHM — EXACT UPDATE RULES AS WRITTEN IN THE PAPER
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

3.1 Unfairness estimate uf_i^t — Eq. (1)

  uf_i^t = { Acc~^t - Acc_i^t ,   if Acc~^t > Acc_i^t
           { 0,                   otherwise

  where:
  - Acc_i^t is the evaluated LOCAL accuracy of client i in round t
    (i.e., accuracy of the current global model θ^t evaluated on client
    i's own local data).
  - Acc~^t (written in the paper with a tilde over "Acc", estimated
    global accuracy) = sum_{i=1}^{m} ω_i^t * Acc~_i^t  — i.e. the
    ESTIMATED global model accuracy in round t, computed as the
    aggregation-weight-weighted sum of each selected client's local
    training accuracy Acc~_i^t (tilde), where m is the number of
    selected clients.
  - The paper explicitly notes: the server does NOT have access to
    validation data in this setting (realistic assumption), so the
    "global" accuracy used here is only an ESTIMATE, not a true
    held-out evaluation.
  - uf_i^t is described as "the unfairness level of client i in round
    t, which is a cumulative value that reflects whether client i has
    been treated fairly or not so far" — NOTE: despite this wording in
    the prose, Eq. (1) itself defines uf_i^t as a PER-ROUND
    (non-cumulative) quantity — the clipped (non-negative) gap between
    the estimated global accuracy and client i's own local accuracy for
    round t. The cumulative aspect is carried by the separate queue
    Q_i(t) in Eq. (2), not by uf_i^t itself. (This is exactly as the
    paper writes it — flagging the apparent terminology tension rather
    than resolving it, since the paper does not resolve it either.)

3.2 Queue update Q_i(t) — Eq. (2)

  Q_i(t) = max{ Q_i(t-1) + α * uf_i^t - ω_i^t · 1_[x_i(t-1)=1] ,  0 }

  where:
  - x_i(t-1) ∈ {0,1} indicates whether client i was selected in round
    (t-1) (1 = yes, 0 = no).
  - 1_[condition] is an indicator function (1 if condition true, else 0).
  - α is a hyper-parameter that controls fairness (the paper calls it
    "a hyper-parameter that controls fairness").
  - ω_i^t is the aggregation weight of client i in round t (defined in
    Eq. 3 below) — used here as the "penalty" subtracted off when the
    client WAS selected in the previous round.

  Design rationale, quoted/paraphrased directly from the paper (Section
  3.3), to "facilitate vulnerable clients":
  - For clients with LOW ACCURACY: Q_i(t) is a cumulative value
    reflecting overall unfairness level each round.
  - For clients who are NOT SELECTED: the indicator 1_[condition] is 0,
    so they get the full unfairness increment α·uf_i^t added with no
    offsetting penalty term.
  - For clients with SMALL WEIGHT: the cumulative value gets only a
    small penalty (-ω_i^t), resulting in a relatively high Q_i(t).

  I.e., a client who has not been treated fairly (low-accuracy, not
  selected, or given small weight) will end up with a higher Q_i(t).

3.3 Aggregation weight ω_i^{t+1} derived from Q_i(t) — Eq. (3)

  ω_i^{t+1} = { n_i / sum_{i=1}^{m} n_i ,        if Q_1(t) = ... = Q_m(t) = 0
              { Q_i(t) / sum_{i=1}^{m} Q_i(t) ,  otherwise

  where n_i is the data amount (number of local samples) of client i,
  and Q_i(t) is the cumulative unfairness value from Eq. (2). m is the
  number of selected clients in that round.

  - When all selected clients' cumulative unfairness is 0 (this is the
    case at round 0, by initialization), weights fall back to plain
    data-amount-proportional weighting — exactly standard FedAvg
    weighting.
  - Otherwise, weight is proportional to the client's cumulative
    unfairness Q_i(t): higher unfairness => higher aggregation weight
    => greater influence on the aggregated global model.

3.4 Client selection rule (top-Q vs random fraction r)

  Client selection is described in Section 3.4 text plus the Remark
  below Eq. (3), and spelled out in Algorithm 1:

  - CORE RULE: After updating the accumulated unfairness queue, "the
    server can select the top-m clients with the highest Q_i(t) from
    the overall clients set N to perform local training." This
    guarantees vulnerable clients (higher cumulative unfairness) get
    selected.
  - PROBLEM NOTED BY THE AUTHORS: Selecting purely by top-Q unfairness
    can bias the global model toward vulnerable/rare-data clients,
    which "can be another form of unfairness" and can reduce average
    global accuracy.
  - FIX — hyper-parameter r: the paper introduces r to balance the two
    kinds of unfairness. Of the total m clients selected per round, a
    RANDOM selection method picks a portion r of the clients, and the
    unfairness-based (top-Q) selection method picks the remaining
    (1 - r) portion. (The paper's exact wording: "This is achieved by
    using a random selection method to select a portion of r clients
    and using our client selection method to select the remaining.")
    The range of r is stated as [0, 1] (Section 4.4).
  - At round t = 0: queues are initialized Q_1(0) = Q_2(0) = ... =
    Q_N(0) = 0, so with all Q equal, the top-m selection reduces to a
    (effectively) random selection, and initial aggregation weight is
    set directly as w_i^{t+1} = n_i / sum_{i=1}^{m} n_i (data-amount
    proportional) — see Algorithm 1 pseudocode.

  Remark given by the authors on the special-case relationship to
  FedAvg: "The FedAvg algorithm can be seen as a special case of our
  FCFL. When α in Eq. (2) is 0, the cumulative unfairness value of all
  clients Q_i(t) is 0 in every round. Hence, client selection is random
  and the aggregation weight is proportional to the amount of data. As
  α increases, the unfairness uf_i^t imposes more influence in Q_i(t),
  which will have a higher chance of being selected and receiving
  higher aggregation weights, thus improving the fairness of FL."

3.5 Full Algorithm 1 pseudocode (as given, transcribed faithfully)

  Algorithm 1: Fairness Compensation Federated Learning (FCFL)
  Input: clients set N; communication rounds T; local epochs E;
         learning rate η
  Output: global model θ^{T+1}

  Server executes:
    initialize global model θ^0
    for each round t = 0, 1, 2, ..., T do
        distribute global model θ^t to all clients
        all clients evaluate θ^t and upload local accuracy {Acc_i^t | i ∈ N}
        to the server
        if t == 0 then
            initialize unfairness queues: Q_1(0) = Q_2(0) = ... = Q_N(0) = 0
            select m clients to constitute subset S_t according to the
            selection method
            initialize the aggregation weight w_i^{t+1} = n_i / Σ_{i=1}^m n_i,
            i ∈ S_t
        else
            calculate the unfairness of all clients via Eq. (1)
            update the accumulated unfairness queue via Eq. (2)
            select m clients to constitute subset S_t according to the
            selection method
            calculate the aggregation weight via Eq. (3)
        end if
        for each client i ∈ S_t do
            θ_i^{t+1}, Acc~_i^{t+1} ← ClientUpdate(i, θ^t)
        end for
        update global model parameters:
            θ^{t+1} = Σ_{i=1}^m ω_i^{t+1} θ_i^{t+1}
        estimate global performance:
            Acc~^{t+1} = Σ_{i=1}^m ω_i^{t+1} Acc~_i^{t+1}
    end for

  ClientUpdate(i, θ^t): // Run on client i
      client i ∈ S_t updates θ^t for E epochs with step size η to obtain
      θ_i^{t+1}
      client i ∈ S_t evaluates θ_i^{t+1} on local datasets to obtain
      Acc~_i^{t+1}
      client sends θ_i^{t+1} and Acc~_i^{t+1} to the server

3.6 Communication/computation overhead notes (Section 3.5)

  - FCFL adds a small communication overhead vs FedAvg: each client
    uploads local accuracy Acc_i^t once per round ("8 more bits per
    round" per the paper's own estimate for that scalar), plus the
    client-side local-training-accuracy upload used for the global
    accuracy estimate. Paper's claim: "our FCFL only involves a few
    more bits of communication cost per round."
  - Computation: clients need one extra accuracy evaluation of the
    global model on local data (same cost class as FedAvg's existing
    computations); to save cost, the paper suggests this accuracy can
    be estimated via mini-batch samples rather than the full local
    dataset. Server-side computation (unfairness, cumulative
    unfairness, aggregated weights, global performance estimate) is
    "simple arithmetic operations within O(N)" and considered
    negligible given typical server compute power.

4.2 Non-IID data partitioning — EXACT QUOTE

  "We only consider non-IID scenarios since heterogeneous non-IID data
  distribution is the reason for performance fairness. To simulate this
  scenario, we design three settings to allocate data to the clients.
  (1) We sort all data samples based on labels and then split them into
  200 shards, where each client randomly picks 2 shards without
  replacement. (2) We utilize the Dirichlet function to set different
  levels of non-IID local clients [23]. (3) In The Complete Works of
  William Shakespeare [15], each speaking role in each play is treated
  as a device, We subsample 31 speaking roles following the setting in
  [1]."

  Notes:

    replacement) is the classic non-IID shard partition popularized by
    McMahan et al.'s original FedAvg paper, applied here — presumably
    to MNIST and/or CIFAR-10 (the paper does not explicitly map each
    of the three settings (1)/(2)/(3) one-to-one onto MNIST vs CIFAR-10
    vs Shakespeare beyond (3) clearly being for Shakespeare; settings
    (1) and (2) both appear usable for MNIST/CIFAR-10 and the text does
    not explicitly disambiguate which of (1) or (2) was used for which

    explicitly in the paper, so do not assume one over the other).
  - Setting (2) (Dirichlet partition) is also the method illustrated
    earlier in Fig. 2's MNIST example (20 clients, Dirichlet function),
    used there purely as an illustrative figure, not necessarily the

  - No explicit Dirichlet concentration/skew parameter (e.g., no α_Dir
    or β value) is stated anywhere in the paper for the non-IID

4.8 Hyperparameter sensitivity — effect of r (Section 4.4, Fig. 5)

  - r (client selection ratio handled by RANDOM selection, vs. the
    remaining 1-r by unfairness/top-Q selection) is swept over its
    full range [0, 1].
  - Theoretical expectation stated by authors: increasing r increases
    accuracy (more randomness lets the global model see more clients'
    data) but also increases variance among clients (fewer vulnerable
    clients prioritized).
  - MNIST (Fig. 5a): due to MNIST's simplicity, performance
    distribution per client is already fairly uniform; average
    precision and variance do NOT show significant change as r varies.
  - CIFAR-10 (Fig. 5b): effect is clearly visible — average accuracy
    AND variance both increase as r increases, confirming the
    theoretical expectation.
  - Parameter r was tuned via grid search. r = 0.6 was selected as "the
    best value that can reach a perfect trade-off between accuracy and
    variance (i.e., fairness)" — this matches the r=0.6 used for FCFL's
    CIFAR-10 and Shakespeare rows in Table 2 (MNIST's best row instead
    used r=0.4, selected presumably because of MNIST's low sensitivity
    to r per the grid search / Fig 5a finding, though the paper does
    not explicitly restate this reasoning for why MNIST's optimal r
    differs).

4.9 Ablation experiments (Section 4.5, Table 4) — CIFAR-10 only

  Two variants compared against full FCFL, all on CIFAR-10:
  - FCFL|RS: replaces unfairness-based CLIENT SELECTION with pure
    random selection (keeps unfairness-based reweighting).
  - FCFL|DAR: replaces unfairness-based REWEIGHTING with data-amount
    reweighting (keeps unfairness-based client selection).

  Table 4 "Ablation studies of FCFL on CIFAR-10":
  Columns: Method | Accuracy | Best 10% | Worst 10% | Variance

    FCFL     : 46.12 | 65.39 | 28.03 | 114.59
    FCFL|RS  : 46.06 | 65.60 | 22.91 | 151.31
    FCFL|DAR : 43.56 | 61.00 | 26.57 | 96.31

  Findings stated by authors:
  - FCFL vs FCFL|RS: unfairness-based SELECTION significantly improves
    worst-10% performance (28.03 vs 22.91) and reduces variance (114.59
    vs 151.31) — shows unfairness-based client selection effectively
    helps the unfairness issue.
  - FCFL vs FCFL|DAR: FCFL|DAR achieves much LOWER variance (96.31,
    even lower than full FCFL's 114.59) but at the cost of global
    accuracy (43.56 vs 46.12) and best-10% accuracy (61.00 vs 65.39) —
    i.e., pure unfairness-based reweighting alone (without the
    data-amount fallback mechanism) over-sacrifices average/majority
    performance. The authors interpret this as showing unfairness-based
    REWEIGHTING (as designed in FCFL, i.e. the Eq. 3 two-branch form
    with the data-amount fallback) "can improve accuracy effectively"
    relative to a naive always-unfairness-weighted alternative.
  - Overall conclusion: full FCFL "takes advantage of both client
    selection and reweighting strategy, providing a well-justified
    fairness and performance in FL."

5.5 Explicit statement on "fairness metric" terminology
  Throughout, "the fairness metric" that the 30.4% improvement figure
  (abstract/conclusion) refers to is VARIANCE of per-client test
  accuracy (as defined/used throughout Section 4.2 and Table 2) — not
  any other named fairness metric (e.g., not Gini coefficient, not a
  Jain's fairness index — the paper does not use those terms for its
  own primary metric; those are mentioned only as other methods' names,
  e.g. FedGini, GIFAIR which internally may use Gini-like penalties,
  but the paper's own reported comparison metric is variance).
