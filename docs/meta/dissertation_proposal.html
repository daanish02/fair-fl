Where fair FL sits in 2026

Understand this before walking into the meeting. It tells you where the field is, what's been done, and where the gaps are.

What federated learning actually is
Multiple clients (hospitals, phones, banks) each train a model locally on their own data. A central server aggregates their model updates — not the data — into one global model. The data never leaves each client. The standard algorithm is FedAvg (McMahan et al., 2017): clients do local gradient steps, send model weights to server, server averages them, sends back.
Why fairness is a problem in FL specifically
In standard FL, clients with more data or faster compute dominate the global model. Minority groups, rare demographics, or low-resource clients get worse model performance — even though they participate in training. This is both a technical problem (unequal loss across clients) and a societal one (certain groups get discriminated against by the resulting model). The problem is worse in FL than in centralised ML because you can't see the data, you can only see the model updates.
What exists already
Current approaches to FL fairness
q-FedAvg — reweights clients by their loss so worse-performing clients get more influence in aggregation.

Ditto — trains a personalised local model alongside the global model, improving per-client fairness.

FPFL — frames fairness as a constrained optimisation problem.

FairFedCS — adjusts client selection probabilities dynamically using Lyapunov optimisation.

FedGraph-Fair — combines dynamic client graphs with primal-dual fairness objectives (2025).
What's still missing
Open problems — where your work goes
Runtime fairness — all existing methods set constraints at design time. When distributions shift, they fail silently.

Agentic orchestration — no work uses autonomous agents to manage fairness adaptively. Proposed as a direction in arXiv:2604.04895 (2026) but not implemented.

Multi-level fairness — fairness between clients AND fairness within clients (across demographic groups in each client's data) simultaneously.

Fairness under adversarial clients — what happens to fairness guarantees when some clients are malicious?

The core problem you are solving

Be able to state this in one sentence. Everything else in your dissertation flows from this.

One-sentence problem statement
"Fairness constraints in federated learning are enforced statically at design time, making them brittle under distribution shift, client heterogeneity, and adversarial participation — and no existing work uses adaptive agent-based mechanisms to enforce fairness dynamically at runtime."
Why static fairness fails
Distribution shift — client data distributions change over time (seasonal data, user behaviour changes). A fairness constraint calibrated at round 1 may actively harm minority groups by round 100.

Client heterogeneity — new clients join with different demographic compositions. Static weights designed for the original client set become misaligned.

Non-IID data — clients have fundamentally different data. Averaging a fixed fairness weight across them treats unequal clients equally — which is itself unfair.
Why agents are the right solution
Observation — an agent can observe per-client fairness metrics each round, something static protocols ignore after setup.

Reasoning — an agent can contextualise why fairness is degrading (new client? data shift? adversarial update?) and respond appropriately to each cause.

Adaptation — an agent can adjust client selection, aggregation weights, and privacy budgets simultaneously and dynamically — a unified intervention that static methods cannot perform.

Your edge — you have actually built agentic systems professionally. Most FL researchers have not.
Key technical concepts to know before the meeting
Fairness definitions you should be able to explain
Group fairness / demographic parity — the model's positive prediction rate should be equal across demographic groups (e.g. gender, race). Most commonly used in FL fairness literature.
Equalized odds — equal true positive AND false positive rates across groups. Stronger than demographic parity. More appropriate for high-stakes decisions.
Client-level fairness — each FL client (hospital, device) gets similar model performance regardless of how much data they contributed.
Individual fairness — similar individuals get similar predictions. Harder to enforce in FL because you can't see individual data across clients.

Three concrete research directions to propose

Go in with options, not just one idea. Let the professor help you choose based on feasibility within your semester timeline. Present these in order — Option 1 is your preference.

Option 1 — your preference, highest impact
Agent-based runtime fairness enforcement in FL
Estimated scope: 4–5 months · Difficulty: Medium-High · Paper potential: Strong
What you build: A simple rule-based agent that sits at the FL server. Each round, it observes per-client loss disparity and a fairness metric (e.g. demographic parity gap). When fairness degrades beyond a threshold, the agent dynamically adjusts: (a) client selection weights — upweighting underrepresented clients, (b) aggregation coefficients — giving more weight to clients whose updates improve fairness, (c) optionally, local training epochs per client.

Why it's feasible for a Masters: You start with a simple rule-based agent, not a learned one. Rule-based agents are fast to implement and still produce a meaningful research contribution when compared to static baselines. If time allows, you extend to a learned policy (Q-learning or a small neural policy).

What makes it novel: No published paper implements agent-based orchestration specifically for fairness enforcement at runtime. arXiv:2604.04895 (April 2026) proposes this paradigm but does not implement it. You would be the first empirical implementation.

Baselines to beat: FedAvg (no fairness), q-FedAvg, Ditto. Beat all three on fairness metrics without unacceptable accuracy loss.

Dataset: Adult Income dataset or COMPAS — standard fairness benchmarks. Simulate as FL using Flower framework (flower.dev). Non-IID split across clients using Dirichlet distribution.
Option 2 — solid, slightly more conservative
Fairness-aware client selection under heterogeneous data distributions
Estimated scope: 3–4 months · Difficulty: Medium · Paper potential: Good
What you build: A client selection mechanism that considers both model performance and fairness when choosing which clients participate in each FL round. Existing work (FairFedCS) uses Lyapunov optimisation. Your approach: use a lightweight scoring agent that ranks clients by their estimated fairness contribution — clients whose data would improve demographic parity get higher selection probability.

Why it's feasible: Client selection is a well-defined, bounded problem. The implementation scope is more contained than full orchestration. Strong baseline literature exists to compare against (FairFedCS, random selection, contribution-based selection).

What makes it novel: Explicitly framing client selection as a fairness intervention rather than just a performance optimisation. Adding the agent-based adaptive element on top of existing scoring methods.

Connection to Option 1: This can be framed as a component of the larger Option 1 system — your dissertation does client selection, the PhD work does the full orchestration stack. Natural progression.
Option 3 — most feasible, if timeline is tight
Privacy-fairness tradeoff analysis in FL with adaptive DP budgets
Estimated scope: 3 months · Difficulty: Medium · Paper potential: Good, theory-heavy
What you build: An empirical and theoretical analysis of how differential privacy noise in FL disproportionately harms minority groups — and a mechanism that allocates privacy budgets per client group to balance the privacy-fairness tradeoff. The agent component: a budget allocation policy that adapts per-group DP noise levels based on observed fairness degradation.

Why it's feasible: Primarily analytical with a focused implementation. The privacy-fairness tradeoff is well-documented theoretically (Kamath et al., ICML 2024) — you are extending it to the FL setting empirically.

What makes it novel: Most FL fairness work ignores privacy entirely. Most FL privacy work ignores fairness. The interaction is an open problem explicitly flagged in the 2026 FL fairness survey (arXiv:2602.00718).

Risk: More theoretical — if you are stronger at building systems than proving theorems, Option 1 or 2 plays more to your strengths.
